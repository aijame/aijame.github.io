Resource and Time-Adjusted Research Productivity Index for Funding Allocations Using Critical Thinking & Ethics in Decision Making

Abstract
Performance-based funding rewards citations and grants already accumulated, not output relative to the resources a researcher actually had. This paper critiques that logic, using Sorzano and Pueche-Granados (2026) as the test case, and proposes the Resource- and Time-Adjusted Research Productivity Index (RTA-RPI): scientific output divided by cumulative resource exposure. RTA-RPI exposes funding efficiency that raw bibliometrics hide, and gives early-career and underfunded researchers a fair basis for comparison. Applied math is only as fair as the assumptions behind it: critical thinking about what a formula leaves out — who had less time, less money, less visibility — matters more than the formula itself, since an unexamined metric just launders existing bias through a number neglecting ethics.


Keywords: Research Funding; Researchers Finance; Scientometrics; Research Ethics; Applied Math; Agentic Computer Simulations



1. Introduction

Funding decisions determine which questions get studied and who gets to study them. Most systems use historical performance — publications, citations, h-index, institutional reputation — as the main signal for who gets funded next.


That signal conflates two different things: how much a researcher has produced, and how much opportunity they had to produce it. A senior professor with decades of multimillion-dollar funding and a researcher with almost none are not comparable on raw output alone.


This is the Matthew effect in science: accumulated advantage compounds, so established researchers who already hold funding, infrastructure, and visibility keep receiving more of each, while early-career researchers and those without substantial initial support fall further behind regardless of underlying ability [2]. A performance-based funding system built on historical output does not correct for this effect; it formalizes it.


Sorzano and Pueche-Granados (2026) propose performance-weighted allocation mechanisms built on exactly this signal [1]. Their framework is useful, but it inherits the conflation: it predicts who will keep producing, not who would produce the most per dollar of opportunity.


Central claim: scientific productivity should be evaluated relative to the resources and time available to generate it — not by accumulated performance alone. The RTA-RPI operationalizes this.



2. The Problem with Performance-Weighted Funding

Prediction ≠ policy. Sorzano and Pueche-Granados show historical performance predicts future performance across ∼4,400 researchers [1]. That does not establish that funding the predicted winners is the fairest or most valuable use of money — prediction and allocation are different questions.


No resource denominator. Their performance signal is never divided by what it cost to produce. A researcher with $10M and 20 years is compared to one with $5K and 2 years on the same scale, which rewards access to money as if it were skill.


Concentration. Their own stochastic model, optimized for impact alone, converges to funding just 5 of ∼4,400 researchers. The authors admit fairness and diversity must be imposed as separate constraints — they don't emerge from the objective. An allocation can be mathematically optimal and still inequitable.


Early-career penalty. Qualified new researchers must out-compete decades of institutional advantage just to get their first independent funding. Historical performance shouldn't be the sole gate to that opportunity.



3. The RTA-RPI

Let Pi be researcher i's field-normalized scientific performance (initially: citations), Ri(t) their annual accessible research resources, and Ti their active research period. Cumulative resource exposure (Eq. 1) is defined as:

Ei = ∫0Ti Ri(t) dt (1)

When a researcher has documented career breaks (parental leave, illness, or other interruptions), exposure is instead computed discretely over active periods only (Eq. 2):

Ei = ∑t=1Ti Ri(t) ai(t) (2)

where ai(t) = 1 if researcher i was active in year t, and 0 during a documented break — so break years contribute zero exposure rather than diluting the researcher's average.

The index itself (Eq. 3) is performance per $1,000 of cumulative exposure:

RTA‑RPIi = (Pi / Ei) × 1000 (3)

When annual resources are roughly constant, Eq. 3 reduces to Pi ÷ (Ri × Ti) × 1000.

A higher score means more output per dollar of opportunity — it does not by itself prove higher quality, or that more funding would scale proportionally. It adds a dimension absolute performance rankings omit.

Table 1. Illustrative RTA-RPI comparison across ten simulated researcher profiles.


With 10 profiles, the RTA-RPI spread runs from 0.044 (Case G) to 25.0 (Case J) — a ∼570× range, while raw citations only range ∼200× (50 to 10,000). Case G and Case J anchor the two extremes and are the clearest cases to explain the index by. Three patterns stand out:


Scale breaks the raw-citation ranking. Case G has the most citations (10,000) of anyone in the set but the lowest RTA-RPI (0.044) — $225M bought that output, 2.5× Case A's exposure for 2.5× Case A's citations, so efficiency is essentially flat between them despite Case G looking far more “productive” on paper. Case G is the model's cautionary example: raw scale without a resource denominator overstates productivity.


Time dilutes the index, not just money. Case B and Case J share the same near-zero funding rate ($5K and $2K/yr) but Case J's single-year window gives it the highest score in the set (25.0) — short, cheap, high-output stints score best; Case F and Case C show the inverse: real output stretched over decades of low funding (1.6 and 1.33) lands mid-table, not at the top, because years accumulate in the denominator even when dollars don't. Case J is therefore the model's open question: it is the index's top performer and its clearest case for a minimum-exposure safeguard (Section 6), since a single lucky year should not outscore a lifetime of comparable efficiency.


Mid-tier funding does not guarantee mid-tier efficiency. Case D, Case E and Case I all sit in the $300K–$2M/yr band but span 0.06–0.67, a >10× difference — Case E's heavy short-term funding is the least efficient of the three; Case I's lower, longer, steadier funding is the most. Funding level alone does not predict RTA-RPI; the citations-per-dollar-year ratio does.


Overall, RTA-RPI and raw citations rank researchers almost oppositely at the extremes (compare Case A and Case G at the bottom vs. Case B and Case J at the top), which is exactly the inversion the index is built to surface.



5. Application to Funding Allocation

RTA-RPI is a funding-efficiency signal, not a universal ranking. Decision procedure:

  1. Eligibility gate — scientific quality, feasibility, integrity, ethics, independent of RTA-RPI.
  2. Resource exposure — estimate cumulative accessible funding per researcher over the evaluation period.
  3. Compute RTA-RPI — field-normalized performance ÷ verified exposure.
  4. Weight toward opportunity — give particular consideration to high-RTA-RPI researchers where more funding would expand independent capacity.
  5. Protect early-career access — a funding pathway that does not force new researchers to out-compete lifetime citation totals. RTA-RPI supports this; it should not become a new mandatory leaderboard.


6. Methodological Safeguards

  • Disciplinary normalization — citation patterns differ by field; use field-normalized, publication-year-adjusted measures (Leiden Manifesto; DORA 2024) [3][4].
  • Resource attribution — separate a PI's direct individual budget from shared infrastructure, personnel, and collaborative resources; attribute transparently.
  • Inflation adjustment — convert all historical funding to constant purchasing power.
  • Career interruptions — adjust the active period for documented leave or illness; measure actual research time, not elapsed calendar time.
  • Citation attribution — use contribution-based, not full, credit on collaborative work.
  • Citation lag — use appropriate citation windows so recent work isn't penalized for having less time to accumulate citations.
  • Minimum exposure threshold — cap or flag extreme RTA-RPI values from very low or undocumented exposure; report as uncertain, not exact.


6.1 Ethical Considerations

Any index that moves real money carries ethical weight beyond its math. Six concerns apply directly to RTA-RPI:

  • Gaming risk — if exposure self-reporting is unverified, researchers have an incentive to understate funding to inflate their score; verification by funders, not self-report, is required.
  • Due process — a low RTA-RPI must never be treated as evidence of low ability; it should only ever narrow or widen opportunity, never serve as public judgment of a researcher's worth.
  • Equity vs. merit tension — correcting for resource inequality is a fairness choice, not a neutral technical one; it should be adopted transparently as policy, not smuggled in as if the formula alone settles what funders owe each other.
  • Citations without context is a double failure — a senior researcher citing raw citation counts while ignoring decades of funding and time behind them is not just ethically unfair to those with less access; it is mathematically weak, since an unadjusted number conflates opportunity with ability and so proves less than it appears to.
  • Academic exploitation and bullying — failure to account for indexes like RTA-RPI leaves resource-adjusted output invisible to institutions, which lets senior researchers over-pressure early-career researchers, including postdoctoral researchers and PhD-level students, into producing under funding and time conditions that would be recognized as inequitable if measured. This dynamic is the specific subject of a parallel case study on postdoctoral exploitation tactics in AI-era academia [5].
  • Self-citation adjustment — under the simple form of RTA-RPI, citations a researcher makes to their own prior work should be subtracted from Pi before the index is computed, so self-citation cannot inflate the numerator; this revised scoring keeps RTA-RPI aligned with externally recognized contribution rather than self-generated citation count.


7. Limitations

  • Not a causal claim. A high RTA-RPI may reflect personal effort, collaboration, or access to tools built by others, not funding efficiency alone; the index measures output relative to resources, but does not prove that the resources caused the output.
  • Citations ≠ quality. Citation count says nothing about originality, reproducibility, or real-world impact, so a high RTA-RPI does not mean the underlying research is better — only that it was produced with less money and time.
  • Funding buys more than citations. Grants also fund infrastructure, train doctoral students, and transfer technology to industry; none of those outcomes are counted by RTA-RPI, which only tracks citation-based performance.
  • Resource exposure is hard to isolate. On large, multi-investigator grants, splitting shared funding fairly between individual researchers is genuinely difficult; an inaccurate exposure estimate distorts the index directly, since exposure sits in the denominator.
  • Not yet tested on real data. The illustrative cases in Section 4 are simulated, not observed; RTA-RPI still needs validation against actual longitudinal funding and citation records before a funder should rely on it.


8. Conclusion

Accumulated performance is not the same as efficient use of opportunity. Sorzano and Pueche-Granados show that impact-maximizing allocation concentrates funding in the already-funded — a mathematically sound outcome that is not necessarily an equitable one. RTA-RPI reframes the question: not how much has a researcher produced, but how much per dollar of opportunity they actually had. Scientific funding should reward contribution and resource efficiency — not simply finance the continuation of accumulated advantage.
